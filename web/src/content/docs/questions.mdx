Twelve is not a finding about language. It is an interface decision from 2013 — a quiz that fits on a page and holds attention — and it has been inherited ever since as though it were a property of the problem. It is worth asking what the number should be, and the honest way to ask is to measure error against how many questions are asked and look at the curve.

## Two curves behave, one does not

Two of these curves do what you would expect: more questions, less error, still falling when the measurement runs out at <Stat n={kMax} />. At that point the network is down to <Stat>{km(twelve.netEnd.medianKm)}</Stat> of median error and the undiscounted Bayesian model to <Stat>{km(twelve.correctEnd.medianKm)}</Stat>.

## Why the count and the discount cannot be settled separately

So the question count and the discount cannot be settled separately. With the discount in place, asking more than about <Stat n={twelve.best.k} /> questions is actively harmful, which makes twelve look like a reasonable choice for the wrong reason. Remove it and the ceiling disappears; the curve is still falling at the end of the measurement, and where to stop becomes a judgement about how long a stranger will keep answering, not about statistics.

This quiz asks <Stat n={constants.nQuestions} />, and that is the judgement rather than a result. It is the point at which an extra question stops being worth about 30 km of accuracy — roughly what one more screen of a stranger's patience seems to cost. Ask instead where 90% of the available accuracy has arrived and the answer is further out. Both are defensible; neither is a fact about dialects, which is the entire point of this section.

:::note[These are simulated speakers]
They are built to violate the model's assumptions in three calibrated ways rather than to obey them. That makes the curve an answer to "how wrong can this be and still work" rather than a measurement of accuracy on real people, which this project does not have and [says so plainly](/american-dialects/limits/).
:::
